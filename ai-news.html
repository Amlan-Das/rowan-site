AI news

Nobody really knows where AI is going, including the people building it. Most technologies show you their shape before they change anything. This one keeps surprising its own makers, and it has already become one of the forces behind almost everything happening right now: where the money goes, where the power plants go, what gets built, who gets hired, who gets nervous.

We are arranging the world around something we cannot predict. Maybe that is simply what living through a turning point feels like: plenty of headlines and very little explanation.

How a machine learns

Every AI system you read about above is, underneath, a stack of numbers that gets nudged every time it is wrong. The version below has a single layer of them. It reads handwritten digits, and it learns the way the big ones do: guess, check, correct, repeat.

The numbers live in a table called W, for weights. The picture it is looking at is a list called x. Nothing else is needed: it is about thirty lines of Python, and you can train it right here.

  1. 1

    Look

    Turn the picture into x: one number per pixel, 1 where there is ink and 0 where there is not. A 28 by 28 picture becomes 784 ones and zeros.

  2. 2

    Score

    W has one row of weights for each digit. Add up that row’s weights wherever x has ink. That is the digit’s score. The biggest score is the guess.

  3. 3

    Check

    Compare the guess with the right answer, y. This is the only moment the model is told anything about the world.

  4. 4

    Correct

    Add x to the right row and subtract x from the guessed row. Do it for the next picture, and the next, tens of thousands of times.

What to take from it

Learning is repeated correction. The model is never told what a 7 looks like. It guesses, is told it was wrong, and moves the weights a little. After 60,000 pictures a 7-shaped pattern has built up in row 7.

Training and testing are different pictures. Training runs over 60,000 images, three times, so 180,000 corrections. The 10,000 test images only measure the result, which is how you know it learned handwriting rather than memorised pictures.

This is the smallest case. Modern models stack many layers of W, add a step that lets them bend the lines between classes, and replace “add or subtract x” with calculus that works out how much each weight caused the mistake. They also have billions of weights rather than 7,840. The loop is the same: score, compare, adjust.
