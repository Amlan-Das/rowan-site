## Opening statement

Mining is the science, technique and business of finding mineral deposits and getting them out of the ground at a profit. Metallurgy, the study of metals and their properties, covers what happens next: turning rock into metal someone will pay for. It still sits underneath most of the economy, from the copper in a power line to the iron in a steel beam. Miners are price takers. They sell into global markets they can't control, so earnings rise and fall with the commodity and the economy. In 2025 the 40 largest miners grew revenue only 3.3% to US$909bn, but EBITDA jumped 23% to US$248bn as gold and copper prices climbed. That sums up the business: volumes barely move, prices do. It is also important to mention that resources are finite, hence firms intuitively have a finite life-span.

## Key terms

- **Exploration:** the search for new deposits, starting with a desktop study of existing maps, surveys, old drill results and satellite data before anyone goes into the field.
- **Ore:** rock that holds enough valuable mineral to be worth mining and processing at current prices. The same rock can be ore at US$14,000 copper and waste at US$6,000.
- **Resource vs. reserve:** a resource is mineralization with reasonable prospects of being mined at a profit; a reserve is the part a study shows can actually be mined economically.

## Industry characteristics

- **Cyclical price takers:** revenue follows the commodity and economic cycle, so value depends on where we are in the cycle and how volatile prices, earnings and cash flow are expected to be.
- **High fixed costs and debt:** a mine costs about the same to run whether prices are high or low, so small price moves swing operating income. Many miners borrow to build, so equity values and debt ratios swing too.
- **Long lead times:** discovery to production averaged 15.7 years across 127 mines (S&P Global), with financing, permitting, geology, metallurgy and economic risk along the way.
- **Country risk:** deposits are where they are, often in places with political, economic or geographic risk: royalty hikes, export bans or no roads and power.
- **Scarcity:** every tonne mined is a tonne gone, so without new exploration production would fall away quickly.

**Metal groups and what they are used for**

| Group | Examples | Main uses | What drives the price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Precious | Gold, silver, platinum group | Investment and central bank reserves, jewellery, electronics, catalytic converters | Real interest rates, the U.S. dollar, central bank buying, fear |
| Base | Copper, nickel, zinc, lead, aluminium | Wire and power grids, construction, EVs and batteries, galvanizing steel | Industrial output, construction and China |
| Bulk | Iron ore, metallurgical and thermal coal | Steelmaking and power | Chinese steel output and infrastructure |
| Minor / critical | Cobalt, lithium, graphite, silicon, rare earths | Batteries, magnets, chips and solar | EV and tech demand, policy, Chinese supply |

## Mine life cycle

A mine gets built in stages, and each stage either adds confidence or kills the project. Most deposits never become mines. Value is created as risk comes out, which is why a junior's share price can double or halve on a single drill result or study.

**The study pipeline.** Before a company commits to building, it works through a series of studies, each more detailed and expensive than the last.

- **Scoping study:** a first look at whether the deposit could work. In Canada this is a preliminary economic assessment (PEA), and it can lean on inferred resources.
- **Pre-feasibility study:** compares mining and processing options and picks one. It is the first stage at which reserves can be declared.
- **Feasibility study:** detailed engineering, costs and schedule for the chosen plan.
- **Bankable feasibility study:** a feasibility study detailed enough that lenders will finance the build against it.

**Resources and reserves.** Under Canada's NI 43-101 rules and the CIM definitions, resources are ranked by geological confidence. Inferred resources rest on limited drilling, indicated resources on enough drilling to estimate grade and tonnage reasonably well, and measured resources on close-spaced drilling that confirms the deposit is continuous. Before a production decision, resources have to be proven up: indicated resources can become probable reserves and measured resources become proven reserves, once a study shows they can be mined at a profit. Inferred resources can't be converted at all.

| Resource | Confidence* | Can become |
|---|---|---|
| Inferred | ~10%+ | Nothing yet |
| Indicated | ~50%+ | Probable reserve |
| Measured | ~90%+ | Proven reserve |

*Rough chance the mineralization is there. A rule of thumb, not part of the CIM definitions.*

**Exploration properties.** An exploration property is one where no deposit has been found yet. It has no cash flow and often no assets beyond the claims and the data, so its value is the chance of a discovery. That is why exploration companies are valued on spending and comparable deals rather than a DCF (see Valuation). Exploration matters for the whole industry: the more a company mines, the less it has left, and without new discoveries production would cease quite quickly.

![Years from discovery to production: 6.0 for mines started in the 1990s, 15.7 average across 127 mines, 17.8 for mines started 2020-24](primers/images/mining-discovery.png)

*The 1990s figure is approximate (S&P: 2020-24 is nearly three times longer).*

**How a mine makes money.** Revenue = commodity price x annual volume. Volume depends on the rate of ore production, the grade of that ore and metallurgical recovery, the share of metal the plant actually gets out. Price is set by the market, so the levers a miner controls are tonnes, grade, recovery and cost.

**Types of mining.**

- **Surface (open pit):** digs from the top down and suits large, shallow, lower-grade deposits.
- **Underground:** reaches deeper, higher-grade ore through shafts and ramps. The deepest gold mines in South Africa work about 4 km down, still only the top of a crust that is roughly 7 km thick under the oceans and 35 km under the continents.
- **Placer:** uses water and gravity to separate heavy minerals like gold from loose sand and gravel.
- **In-situ:** pumps fluid into the deposit through wells and brings the dissolved metal back up, common for uranium.
- **Dredging:** recovers ore from under water, such as river or offshore sands.

## Valuation

**There is no single method.** How you value a mining asset depends on how far along it is. An exploration property has no cash flow to discount, so it is valued on what has been spent on it and what similar ground has sold for. A producing mine is valued mostly on the cash it will generate over the life of its reserves. Canada's CIMVal standards group the methods into three approaches: income, market and cost.

| Approach | Method | Exploration | Development | Production |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Income | Discounted cash flow | Not generally used | Widely used | Widely used |
| | Real options | Less widely used | Quite widely used | Quite widely used |
| | Monte Carlo analysis | Less widely used | Less widely used | Less widely used |
| | Probabilistic methods | Not widely used | Not widely used | Not widely used |
| Market | Comparable transactions | Widely used | Widely used | Widely used |
| | Option agreement terms | Widely used | Widely used | Quite widely used |
| | Value per unit area | Widely used | Not widely used | Not widely used |
| Cost | Appraised value | Quite widely used | Not widely used | Not generally used |
| | Multiples | Quite widely used | Quite widely used | Widely used |
| | Geoscience factor | Not widely used | Not widely used | Not generally used |

Three methods do not depend on the stage. Net metal value per unit of metal is a widely used rule of thumb, market capitalization is mainly used for single-property junior companies, and gross in-situ metal value is not acceptable. *Valuation methods after Svetlana Baurens and the University of Zurich.*

**Income approach: DCF and NAV.** For a producing or near-production mine, analysts build a DCF over the life of the reserves and add up the projects into a net asset value (NAV). The DCF only works if the price deck is right, which is why the long-term trend matters more than today's price.

**Real options.** An undeveloped deposit works like a call option: the owner can pay the build cost to get the mine's value, but doesn't have to and can wait. The investment can't be reversed, the future reward is uncertain and it doesn't have to happen right away, which is exactly when options pricing beats a static DCF. Once a mine is running, the right to shut it or mothball it when prices fall works more like a put.

**Market approach.** Comparable transactions give a benchmark, but only a rough one: exploration itself is cyclical, so deals done at the top of the cycle make every later deal look cheap. Common ratios are price to NAV (market cap or adjusted market cap, counting core mining assets only, over NAV) and equity value per unit of resource, such as EV per ounce of gold or per pound of copper.

**Cost approach.** The appraised value method adds retained past expenditures (useful exploration already done) to warranted future costs (a reasonable exploration budget). It suits properties that are actively being explored and have no resource yet.

**Managing the cycle.** Fixating on one base year goes wrong in a predictable way: value off a peak year and you overvalue the company, value off a trough and you undervalue it. The inputs get skewed too, including debt ratios, reinvestment, profitability and even the risk-free rate.

![Earnings over time for an old cycle, a new cycle and the trend line](primers/images/mining-cycle-changes.png)

*Earnings over time. Illustrative, after McKinsey & Company (2000).*

The fix is to normalize: use a long-term price deck and mid-cycle margins so that, over a full cycle, high and low cash flows roughly cancel out. What really drives value is the long-term trend, which for metals is set by urbanization. The hard part is spotting when the trend itself shifts, as in the chart.

## Key macro drivers

**Urbanization sets the trend.** When people move to cities they need housing, power lines, transit and water systems, and all of that is steel, copper and aluminium. That is why China drove the last metals supercycle. Its urban share rose from 36% in 2000 to 66% in 2025, and its share of world base metal demand rose with it: by 2009 China used 45-46% of the world's base metals and 46-49% of its steel, up from about 33% and 34% in 2008. Today it uses about 54% of the world's refined copper. India is now about 36% urban, roughly where China was in 2000, which is why it gets talked about as the next big source of demand.

![China's share of world demand for aluminium, copper, zinc, lead, nickel and steel in 2000, 2008 and 2009](primers/images/mining-china-demand.png)

*Copper is now about 54% (2024).*

![Urban population as a share of total, 2025: India 36, Vietnam 39, Indonesia 59, China 66, U.S. 80, Canada 83](primers/images/mining-urbanization.png)

*China was 36% urban in 2000 and 48% in 2009.*

**Prices, not volumes, are driving profits.** PwC's Top 40 grew revenue just 3.3% in 2025 while EBITDA rose 23%. Gold miners ran EBITDA margins near 71%, copper EBITDA rose 80% to US$13.3bn, and coal revenue fell 10.7%. Gold averaged US$3,432 in 2025, up 44%, peaked at US$5,590 in January 2026 and has since fallen about 25%. Copper hit a record near US$14,900/t in 2026.

**Productivity is stuck.** Output per worker has fallen since 2020 and capital productivity was flat in 2025, even as capex rose to US$95bn. Mining scored lowest of any sector on PwC's AI fitness index, and 40% of mining CEOs say their technology is underdelivering, so AI hasn't shown up in the numbers yet.

**Trade restrictions.** China produces more than half of 18 key minerals and dominates processing. It began licensing seven rare earths in April 2025 and widened controls to graphite anode material and more rare earths in October 2025, then paused the October rules until November 10, 2026. Indonesia, the world's biggest nickel supplier, has banned raw ore exports since 2020.

**Satellites, data and AI in exploration.** Satellite imagery and data tools now produce prospectivity heat maps, recommended drill targets and 3D models of a deposit. It is not a big trend yet, but it could cut the cost and time of finding new deposits.

**Infrastructure, ESG and policy.** A mine is worthless without power lines, roads, rail and a port, so mining spend is tied to infrastructure. ESG matters most in Canada, where Indigenous consultation and permitting decide timelines. Policy is now a deciding factor, with governments treating critical minerals as a security issue.
