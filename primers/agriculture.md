Agriculture is one of the oldest industries in the world, and almost every economy still runs on it. Farmland has held its value for centuries, which is why pensions and endowments still own it. Most farmers are price takers. They don't get to set what they pay for seed, fertilizer and fuel, or what they sell their crop for, so profits come down to input costs and when they decide to sell. Farming also takes a lot of capital. Land and equipment last for decades and are mostly bought with debt, so the industry gets hit hard by changes in interest rates, government policy, trade and currency. AI is starting to help, with tools like yield forecasting, satellite crop tracking and precision equipment that let farmers grow more per acre and waste less.

## Where the money is

- **Pure-play agriculture and inputs (~C$190bn):** fertilizer, seed and crop protection makers like Nutrien, Mosaic, CF Industries and Corteva. The most direct exposure to crop prices and farm spending.
- **U.S. ag products and services (~US$78bn):** grain merchants and processors like ADM and Bunge, which make money on volumes and processing margins more than on crop prices.
- **Machinery and ag-tech:** Deere, CNH Industrial and AGCO, plus venture-backed ag-tech. The fastest-growing part, but equipment sales follow farm income.

## What moves farm profits

**Interest rates.** Farming runs on debt, so higher rates go straight to margins and push land values down. U.S. farm interest expense is forecast at a record $33bn+ in 2026, about 21% of operating earnings, up from 12% in 2021.

**Government policy.** Rules decide who can buy farmland (Saskatchewan and Quebec restrict non-residents, Australia is more open), and support programs prop up incomes in bad years. The U.S. paid $23bn in trade aid for 2018-19, and direct payments are forecast at $47.4bn in 2026.

**Tariffs and trade.** When an export market closes, the crop stays at home and prices fall. The 1980 Soviet grain embargo cancelled about 17 million tonnes of U.S. sales and Argentina took the business. In 2018, China's 25% tariff cut U.S. soybean exports to China by 74%, from $12.2bn to $3.1bn, and Brazil took the business. China has now pledged to buy 25 million tonnes of U.S. soybeans a year through 2028.

**Currency.** Grain is priced in U.S. dollars, so a weaker Canadian dollar means more revenue for Canadian growers and fertilizer producers, and a stronger one hurts.

> When all four turn at once, it gets ugly. In the early 1980s the prime rate hit 21.5%, farm exports fell more than 20%, farm debt doubled to $215bn and Midwest farmland lost up to 60% of its value. Massey Ferguson, Allis-Chalmers and International Harvester each lost more than $200M in 1982.

## Trends to watch

**Yield per acre.** U.S. corn yields doubled from 91 bushels an acre in 1980 to a record 186.5 in 2025, and soybeans hit a record 53.0. The U.S. gets about twice the world average per acre, so output grows through yield, not more land. USDA cut its 2026 corn forecast to 178.5 in September because of weather.

![Corn yield per acre for the U.S., Canada, the world and Brazil, 1970 to 2024](primers/images/agriculture-corn-yield.png)

**Precision ag and drones.** GPS tools were used on about 40% of U.S. farm acreage by 2019. Spray drones covered about 16.4 million acres in 2025, up 58.7% in a year, because they let farmers spray only the parts of a field that need it.

**Soil health.** Cover crops reached 18.0 million acres in the 2022 Census of Agriculture, up 17% from 2017. They cut erosion and can lower fertilizer needs over time.

**Machinery slowdown.** Farmers are holding off on new equipment. Through August 2026, U.S. tractor sales were down 12.4% and combine sales were down 9.1%.

**Input costs are outrunning revenue.** USDA expects 2026 farm expenses up 4.5% while cash receipts slip 0.3%, led by fuel (+28.8%) and fertilizer (+15.3%). Diesel averaged $5.00 a gallon through September, up 37%, while corn was up only 3%. Diesel and crop prices usually move together (a 0.84 correlation with corn over 2014-2025) because energy feeds into fertilizer, freight and biofuel demand. In 2026 that broke, and the gap is the squeeze farmers are feeling. Cross-border tariffs add to it: Canada's ~$20bn of retaliatory tariffs from September 8 cover combines and header parts.

**By region.** U.S. net farm income is forecast down 2.6% to $158.4bn in 2026. Canada had record cash receipts of C$101.4bn in 2025, but net income was flat at C$8.3bn as canola exports slid. Brazil's soybean crop is forecast at 186 million tonnes and it gains whenever U.S.-China trade breaks down. Australia's farm output value is forecast down 5% to A$99.4bn.

## Who controls the industry

The top four companies in seeds, crop protection, machinery and animal health hold 41% to 61% of global sales, up from 21% to 32% in 1994. They outspend everyone on R&D, own the patents and control the dealer networks, so new competitors have a hard time winning share. That's why they trade more like industrial stocks than farm stocks: they move with farm income, but they keep their share and margins in downturns.

![Top-4 share of global sales over time and by segment](primers/images/agriculture-concentration.png)

- **Machinery, Deere (DE):** about 15% of global machinery sales. One of the few equipment makers to get through the 1980s farm crisis: it paid down debt and modernized its plants in the 1970s, kept interest and overhead under 5% of sales, and backed its dealers through the downturn. Now it's buying into ag-tech: Blue River ($305M, 2017), Bear Flag Robotics ($250M, 2021), and Sentera and GUSS Automation (2025). FY2025 net income was $5.0bn on $45.7bn of revenue.
- **Seeds and crop protection, Corteva (CTVA):** Bayer, Corteva, Syngenta and BASF control 56% of commercial seeds and 61% of pesticides. Corteva owns Pioneer, bought biologicals makers Stoller and Symborg, and is spinning its seed business off as Vylor in Q4 2026.
- **Animal health, Zoetis (ZTS):** the top four hold about half the market. Zoetis leads with $9.5bn of 2025 revenue and a ~28% net margin, with about 70% of sales from pets, which is steadier than livestock.
- **Ag biotech:** still open. Gene editing and biological inputs are being built by startups like Pivot Bio and Pairwise, and the majors will likely buy the winners.

## Deal case study: GardenCore

In May 2026, Pacific Avenue Capital Partners bought Oldcastle Lawn & Garden from CRH for $1.1bn out of its Fund II and renamed it GardenCore. It makes mulch, soil, stone and lime at 55+ U.S. plants with 1,400+ employees. Jefferies and Weil advised the buyer, Sidley handled the debt financing, and BofA ran the sale for CRH.

**How it was funded.** The biggest piece was real estate. GardenCore sold all 43 of its plants to W. P. Carey for $400M and leased them back for 20 years, which covered about 36% of the price. The debt terms, revenue and EBITDA weren't disclosed, so the rest comes from [my model](primers/agriculture/gardencore-lbo-model.xlsx).

| Sources | $M | % of total |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sale-leaseback (W. P. Carey) | 400.0 | 34.8% |
| First lien term loan (SOFR + 5.25%) | 375.0 | 32.7% |
| Sponsor equity (Fund II) | 298.1 | 26.0% |
| ABL revolver, drawn | 75.0 | 6.5% |
| **Total** | **1,148.1** | **100.0%** |

The lease is cheaper than the term loan (a 7.4% cap rate vs. about 9.2%), but the rent goes up every year and can't be paid off early. It cuts EBITDA from about $126M to $96M, and leverage is 4.7x on the debt alone or 5.5x counting the lease.

**Debt waterfall.** Cash left after interest and the 1% term loan amortization pays off the ABL first. Once that's gone, 75% of what's left sweeps the term loan.

**Returns.** Selling in Year 5 at 7.25x EBITDA, about what Pacific Avenue paid, gives a 22.2% IRR and 2.7x MOIC, all from EBITDA growth and paying down debt. Working backward, the fund gets its money back at a 3.6x exit, clears the usual 8% LP hurdle at 4.6x and hits 20% at 6.7x, so there's a lot of room before it loses money. After a 20% carry, LPs would net about 19% before fees.

## Timber and timberland

Timber works like farming on a much longer clock: the land grows a crop, but the crop takes 25 to 75 years instead of one season. It also works a lot like infrastructure. It's a real asset that lasts for decades, it keeps up with inflation, and it has given steady returns over long, slow cycles. [Read the full timber report (PDF, 5 pages)](primers/agriculture/timber-industry-review.pdf).

- **How it makes money:** harvest volume times log price, less the cost of cutting, hauling, roads and replanting. Volume depends on how fast the trees grow, so buyers value a forest off yield tables.
- **MAI and CAI:** the mean annual increment is the average wood a stand has added per year (total volume / age), and the current annual increment is what it adds this year. When CAI falls back to meet MAI, average growth has peaked and it's the classic time to cut. Radiata pine in New Zealand grows about 27 m³ per hectare a year; Ontario's forest grows about 1.3.
- **Two assets:** the land and the trees are valued separately. Under IFRS the trees are a biological asset marked to fair value every year, and appraisers charge a notional rent for the land underneath them.
- **Who owns it:** in Canada 94% of forest land is public, so most companies only hold harvesting rights and pay stumpage. Big investors came in after the 1974 ERISA law pushed U.S. pensions to diversify, and by 2012 institutions had about US$60bn in timberland.

Returns have been steady. Since 1990 the U.S. timberland index (NCREIF) has had only three down years, and none worse than 5.2%. It made 9.5% in 2008 while the S&P 500 lost 37%.

![NCREIF Timberland Index annual total return, 1990 to 2025](primers/images/timber-ncreif-returns.png)

**Why the sector is falling now.** Lumber prices are down a third from 2021 and U.S. housing starts are down 15%, so West Fraser, Canfor and Weyerhaeuser have all lost a third to half of their sales. Canadian mills got hit hardest: duties plus the new 10% tariff on their lumber reached about 45% in 2025 (57.6% for Canfor), B.C.'s 2023 harvest was 42% below the allowable cut, and in August 2026 the U.S. added 50% tariffs on about US$20bn of Canadian goods, including plywood and wood furniture. Weyerhaeuser owns its forests and pays no duty, but its stock still hit a 52-week low of US$18.31 in October 2026, about half its 2022 peak.

## Deal case study: PSP and Kaingaroa

Kaingaroa is New Zealand's largest plantation forest: about 190,000 ha of radiata pine between Rotorua and Taupō, cutting about 4M m³ of logs a year. PSP Investments, which invests for Canada's federal public sector pensions, bought 30% from Harvard in December 2012 and now owns about 55%, with the NZ Super Fund at 42% and local iwi at about 3%.

- **Land and trees owned separately:** the land went back to a group of iwi in a 2008 Treaty settlement. The owners hold a Crown forestry licence to grow and cut the trees and pay the iwi rent.
- **Why PSP bought:** fast growth, a strong local processing industry and access to Asian export markets, in a long-lived asset that keeps up with inflation. With 94% of Canada's forest publicly owned, a Canadian pension that wants to own forest mostly has to look abroad.
- **What it has earned:** the price wasn't disclosed, but NZ Super's stake went from about NZ$300M in 2006 to NZ$954M by 2012, roughly 22% a year (my estimate). In the year to June 2017 the trees were revalued from NZ$2.5bn to NZ$2.8bn.
- **Risks:** China takes 55% of New Zealand's forestry exports, so Chinese construction drives log prices.
