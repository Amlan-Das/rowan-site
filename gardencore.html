How Pacific Avenue paid for GardenCore

In May 2026, Pacific Avenue Capital Partners bought CRH's lawn and garden business for $1.1bn and renamed it GardenCore. This is how the deal was funded, how the debt gets paid down, and what return the fund needs.

Rowan DasOctober 2026Excel modelFull report, page 4

Pacific Avenue paid $1.1bn for GardenCore, but its fund only put in about $298M. The rest came from selling the company's plants and leasing them back, plus a revolver and a private credit loan.

GardenCore makes mulch, soil, stone and lime at more than 55 plants across the U.S. and has over 1,400 employees. It sells private label and branded products to home improvement retailers and garden centers. CRH sold it while clearing out businesses outside its main building materials lines. Pacific Avenue, a Los Angeles firm that focuses on carve-outs, bought it out of its $1.65bn Fund II.

The price, the sale-leaseback, the advisors and the fund are public. The debt terms, revenue and EBITDA are not, so the numbers below come from my model. They are estimates, and every input can be changed in the Excel file.

How it was funded

The biggest single source of cash was real estate. At closing, GardenCore sold all 43 of its owned plants to W. P. Carey for $400M and leased them back for 20 years. That covered about 36% of the price, so debt and equity only had to fund about $700M.

The catch is rent. At a 7.4% cap rate the lease costs about $29.6M a year and goes up every year. That's cheaper than the roughly 9.2% the term loan costs, but it can't be paid off early, and it cuts EBITDA from about $126M to $96M. Leverage is 4.7x on the debt alone and 5.5x once the lease is counted as debt.

Returns and what the fund needs

If Pacific Avenue sells in Year 5 at 7.25x EBITDA, about what it paid, the equity is worth about $813M against $298M put in. That's a 22.2% IRR and 2.7x MOIC, all from EBITDA growth and paying down debt, with no help from a higher multiple.

The better question is how far the exit can fall before the fund is in trouble. The table works backward from each target IRR to the exit multiple it needs. The fund gets its money back at just 3.6x, clears the usual 8% hurdle at 4.6x and hits 20% at 6.7x.
